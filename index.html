Southern California · Winter and spring 2026–27

A near-record El Niño is building. How much rain will Southern California get?

The tropical Pacific is running hotter than in any El Niño since at least 1950, and NOAA expects a very strong event to peak this winter. History says that tilts the odds toward a wet winter and spring in Southern California, but it does not guarantee one. This page sets 130 years of rainfall records beside the latest forecast so you can see both the tilt and the uncertainty.

History

What 130 years of records show

During El Niño, the Pacific jet stream extends and shifts south and east, steering more storms and subtropical moisture into Southern California. The effect is real but loose. El Niños of the same strength have produced both soaking and dry winters [27].

This El Niño

2026–27 against the giants

Since 1950, three El Niños reached the very strong range on NOAA's relative index: 1982–83, 1997–98 and 2015–16. This year's event is already tracking close to them, and NOAA's forecast puts its peak above all three.

Timing and storms

When the rain comes, and how hard

In the strongest El Niño winters, most of the extra rain has arrived from January through March, and spring has often stayed wet. Wet El Niño seasons also bring more days of heavy rain, the days that cause flooding and debris flows.

Across the U.S.

El Niño's footprint across the country

Southern California sits in the wet half of El Niño's classic pattern, which stretches from California across the southern states to Florida. The northern Rockies, Ohio Valley and parts of the Pacific Northwest tend to come out drier.

Caveats

Why a strong El Niño isn't a sure thing

El Niño loads the dice. Weather still rolls them.

Even in the strongest events, the storm-by-storm weather over the Pacific adds a large random component. Studies of past events and large climate-model ensembles find that El Niño explains only part of the year-to-year swing in West Coast rain, roughly a third at most, which is why the ranges on this page are wide [15, 16, 18].

Remember 2015–16

2015–16 was one of the three strongest El Niños on record, yet the South Coast got only about two-thirds of its normal December–May rain while Northern California was wetter. Research points to a persistent ridge off the coast, tropical ocean warmth outside the El Niño region, and plain bad luck [17, 18, 19].

This event may go beyond the record

NOAA gives 70–83% odds of an event stronger than any since 1950. No statistical method can know how rain responds beyond what has been observed. The “If El Niño is stronger than any on record” switch shows how much the answer depends on that assumption. Reconstructions from old ship records suggest 1877–78 and 1888–89 came close, but few rain gauges existed then.

Why we use the relative index

The whole tropical ocean has warmed, which inflates the traditional Niño‑3.4 index (+3.3 °C this week). Since February 2026, NOAA has classified El Niño with a relative index that subtracts the tropical-average warming, because tropical rainfall, which drives the jet stream, responds to how much warmer the Niño‑3.4 region is than its surroundings [1, 5, 6].

The North Pacific is in a cool phase

The Pacific Decadal Oscillation is strongly negative (−1.55 in September). Some research finds El Niño's effects on the West are more reliable when the PDO is positive [12]. In this data set, adding the PDO did not improve hindcasts, but it remains a source of uncertainty this year.

A warmer climate changes the backdrop

Warmer air holds more water, so the heaviest storms can drop more rain, and California is expected to swing more sharply between wet and dry years [20]. Climate models also project more frequent extreme El Niños and shifts in their reach [21, 22]. The model here assumes the historical relationship still holds and does not add a trend.

Be ready

What a wet El Niño winter can bring

Seasonal totals hide the storms that make them. A few strong atmospheric rivers can deliver much of a season's rain in days [23], and the hazards come from those bursts.

Flash floods and debris flows

Burn scars from recent wildfires, including the January 2025 Palisades and Eaton fires, can send mud and boulders downslope after short bursts of intense rain, well below what it takes to flood elsewhere [24]. Heed flash flood and debris flow warnings and evacuation orders.

Coastal erosion and flooding

El Niño winters bring bigger waves and higher sea levels to the California coast. In 2015–16 the West Coast saw some of the largest winter wave energy and beach erosion on record, even without heavy rain in Southern California [13, 14].

Landslides and road hazards

Repeated storms saturate hillsides, and slopes can fail days after the rain stops. Coastal bluffs, canyon roads and areas with past slides are most at risk.

Where to get forecasts and warnings

NWS Los Angeles/Oxnard and San Diego offices; NOAA's seasonal outlooks; Ready.gov flood guidance; USGS post-fire debris-flow hazard maps.

Methods

Data and methods

El Niño index
NOAA's Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI), the official index since February 1, 2026 [1, 5]: the Niño‑3.4 sea-surface temperature anomaly minus the 20°S–20°N tropical-mean anomaly, rescaled to the traditional index's variability, as 3‑month averages. We use the November–January value as each winter's El Niño strength. For 1872–1949, we rebuilt the same index from NOAA ERSSTv5 [9]; it matches the official index with r = 0.992 and an error of 0.10 °C over 1950–2024.
El Niño forecast
NOAA CPC's official RONI outlook issued in October 2026 [2]: November–January median +2.65 °C, 5–95% range +2.00 to +3.31 °C. We approximate it as a normal distribution with a spread (σ) of 0.40 °C. The strength and uncertainty sliders move this distribution. For reference, NOAA's dynamical model ensemble (NMME) points to a relative peak near +3.5 °C [3], and the IRI model plume averages about +3.5 °C on the traditional index [4].
Precipitation
NOAA nClimDiv monthly precipitation for California climate divisions 6 (South Coast) and 7 (Southeast deserts), 1895 to September 2026 [10], and GHCN-Daily station records [11]. Station values flagged by NOAA quality control are removed, and a month counts only if no more than 3 days are missing. Santa Barbara's downtown record has 46 missing months filled from Santa Barbara Airport, scaled by the long-term ratio of the two stations. Seasons are named for the winter they span (1997–98 means December 1997 to May 1998 for the winter-plus-spring season). Normal means the 1991–2020 average.
Statistical model
For each place and season, a gamma regression with a log link: log(mean) = b0 + bLN·min(x, 0) + bEN·max(x, 0), where x is the November–January RONI. This lets El Niño and La Niña have different effects. El Niño winters (x ≥ 0.5) get their own spread, because their outcomes have been more variable. Fitted by maximum likelihood. A simpler single-slope model and models with a PDO term were tested and did not improve cross-validated skill.
Uncertainty
Each projection combines three sources: the El Niño forecast (strength drawn from the slider's normal distribution), the fitted relationship (150 bootstrap refits that resample seasons), and weather noise (gamma draws). Your browser runs 20,000 Monte Carlo draws each time a setting changes.
Validation
Leave-one-out cross-validation compares the model with a no-El-Niño climatology using the continuous ranked probability score [25, 26].
Limitations
Few strong El Niños exist to learn from, and this one may exceed them all. Station sites and instruments have changed over time. The model ignores El Niño's spatial flavor, the PDO and long-term trends, and it treats each season's total independently of how the season starts. It is an independent statistical analysis, not an official forecast.
